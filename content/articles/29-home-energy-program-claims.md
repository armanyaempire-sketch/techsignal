Home-energy programs can promise lower bills, improved efficiency or a different way to manage household energy. Comparing them responsibly starts with the assumptions behind the promise.

A savings headline by itself does not tell you what a particular household will save.

The useful approach is to turn the headline into a worksheet: **what is being sold, what assumptions are used, what will it cost, and what evidence supports the estimate?**

## Identify what the program actually is

First determine whether the offer is:

- An educational program.
- A consulting service.
- Equipment.
- Software or monitoring.
- A complete installation package.
- A financing arrangement.
- A combination of several services.

The buying decision changes depending on what is included.

For an equipment package, installation and maintenance may be major costs. For an educational program, the main question may be whether the material provides information rather than physical energy savings.

## Check the baseline assumption

Energy use depends on the home and its circumstances.

Before accepting a savings estimate, ask:

**What is the current baseline?**

**What usage level is assumed?**

**Which energy prices are used?**

**Which equipment is being replaced or added?**

**What behavior changes are required?**

**What happens if actual usage differs from the example?**

The U.S. Department of Energy's consumer guidance explains that home energy audits can help identify energy-use problems and opportunities for efficiency improvements.

That makes an audit or other property-specific assessment a useful reference point when a commercial program is making household savings claims.

## Separate possible savings from guaranteed savings

A forecast is not the same as a guaranteed result.

Classify the claim:

**Example:** an illustration using a hypothetical household.

**Estimate:** a forecast based on stated assumptions.

**Modeled scenario:** an output of a specific calculation model.

**Historical result:** a documented past result.

**Guarantee:** an explicit contractual promise with defined terms.

If the assumptions are not explained, treat the figure as difficult to verify.

## Calculate the complete cost

Record:

- Upfront price.
- Installation.
- Equipment.
- Maintenance.
- Financing cost.
- Subscription fees.
- Replacement requirements.
- Expected useful life.
- Refund or cancellation terms.

Then compare cost with the realistic benefit period.

A system that lowers operating costs may still require a large initial investment. A low monthly payment may also hide a longer contract or financing cost.

Look at the complete agreement rather than focusing on one headline number.

## Check compatibility

For equipment or installation programs, verify:

**Home electrical system**

**Existing equipment**

**Available space**

**Local climate**

**Utility requirements**

**Installation requirements**

**Permits or professional installation requirements where applicable**

Never assume that a general program will work with every property.

## Ask how the savings estimate was calculated

Useful evidence can include:

- Technical specifications.
- Published assumptions.
- Independent measurements.
- Utility documentation.
- Installation requirements.
- A clear methodology for savings estimates.

Ask the seller to show the baseline and the inputs behind a forecast.

A chart showing "save up to X%" without assumptions provides less decision support than a transparent model that lets the buyer understand how the number was produced.

## Compare the alternatives

The relevant alternative may not be another commercial program.

Consider:

**Do nothing for now**

**Improve insulation or air sealing**

**Upgrade an inefficient appliance**

**Use a home energy audit**

**Change a household operating habit**

**Evaluate a different equipment option**

A commercial program should be compared with the other actions that could solve the same problem.

## Understand the purchase terms

Before paying, verify:

**Who is the seller?**

**Who performs installation?**

**Who handles warranty claims?**

**What is refundable?**

**What is recurring?**

**How can the agreement be cancelled?**

**What happens if installation cannot be completed?**

Keep a copy of the final terms.

## Be careful with "guaranteed savings"

"Guaranteed" can mean different things depending on the contract.

Check whether the promise applies to:

- a specific monthly amount,
- a percentage,
- a modelled estimate,
- a reimbursement arrangement,
- a limited period,
- or an outcome that depends on conditions.

Read the actual contractual language instead of relying on the headline.

## Use DOE guidance as a starting reference

The Department of Energy provides consumer guidance on energy efficiency, home energy audits and ways to improve household energy performance.

Those resources are useful for learning the basics and creating questions before evaluating a commercial program.

## A practical comparison worksheet

For each option, record:

**Product/service type**

**Upfront cost**

**Recurring cost**

**Savings assumptions**

**Compatibility**

**Installation**

**Maintenance**

**Warranty**

**Refund terms**

**Evidence**

**Main alternative**

If a critical field is unknown, mark it as unknown rather than filling the gap with an assumption.

## Bottom line

Evaluate a home-energy program from its assumptions outward. Determine what is actually included, how savings are calculated, what the complete cost is, what alternatives exist and whether the program fits the property before accepting a savings claim.
